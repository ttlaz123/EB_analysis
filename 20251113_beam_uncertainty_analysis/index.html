Beam Uncertainty Analysis

2025 Nov 13 — Tongtian (Tom) Liu, Dominic Beck

Introduction

Summary: We consider the impact of beam uncertainties on the ability to recover our multipole dependence parameters. We find that there is a ~20% multiplicative effect on the recovered parameter g in the extreme cases that all 4 BK18 maps experience a 5% beamshift in \(\ell\). Distorting only one of the 4 frequencies approximately halves the effect.

We reproduce the analysis done in Clara's posting, IPR9 extending the beam analysis on BICEP 3 to the Keck spectra.

  • 150: B2bbns

We plot the beam window functions (\(B_{\ell}\)'s) in the pager 3.1 below, as well as the potential errors on those \(B_{\ell}\)'s up to 5% beam broadening as specified in section 3.3 of IPR9.

For a specific map frequency \(\nu\), we consider a disorted beam \(B^{\nu}_{\text{dist}}(\ell)\) that is a slightly shifted version of the original beam \(B^{\nu}_{\text{orig}}(\ell)\):

\[B^{\nu}_{\text{dist}}(\ell) = B^{\nu}_{\text{orig}}(\ell(1+\epsilon))\]

Here, \(\epsilon\) ranges from \(-0.05\) to \(0.05\), representing a beam error shift of up to 5 percent. The shift is in the \(\ell\) direction, meaning the shift in \(B(\ell)\) could be greater or lesser depending on \(\ell\) and \(B(\ell)\).

Effect on Power Spectra

This beam broadening translates to a scaling effect on the power spectra bandpowers. We define the scaling factor for a map at frequency \(\nu\) as the ratio of the disorted to the original beam:

\[ \text{scaling}(\nu) = \frac{B^{\nu}_{\text{dist}}(\ell)}{B^{\nu}_{\text{orig}}(\ell)} = \frac{B^{\nu}_{\text{orig}}(\ell(1+\epsilon))}{B^{\nu}_{\text{orig}}(\ell)} \]

This factor simulates the effect if the power spectra were originally multiplied by the original beam. Therefore, to obtain the disorted power spectrum, we must multiply the original spectrum by this scaling factor.

For a cross-map \(\nu_1 \times \nu_2\):

\[ \text{supfac}^{\nu_1 \times \nu_2}(\ell) = A_\ell \times B_\ell^{\nu_1} B_\ell^{\nu_2} \]

So if the original power spectrum, \(D_{\ell, \text{orig}}^{\nu_1 \times \nu_2}\), was incorrectly calibrated due to the beam uncertainty, the new "disorted" power spectrum becomes:

\[ D^{\nu_1 \times \nu_2}_{\text{dist}}(\ell) = D^{\nu_1 \times \nu_2}_{\text{orig}}(\ell) \times \left( \frac{B^{\nu_1}_{\text{orig}}(\ell(1+\epsilon))}{B^{\nu_1}_{\text{orig}}(\ell)} \right) \times \left( \frac{B^{\nu_2}_{\text{orig}}(\ell(1+\epsilon))}{B^{\nu_2}_{\text{orig}}(\ell)} \right) \]

The disorted power spectrum is obtained by applying the beam scaling factor for each frequency component, \(\nu_1\) and \(\nu_2\), of the cross-spectrum map. We scale our bandpower spectra by calculating the scaling factor for each bin center.

Impact on g

We run the MCMC pipeline for the EB only spectra, varying epsilon and seeing its impact on the recovered g. We also check the effect of only using BICEP3 data (B95lf), as well as the impact of changing the number of bins used. We summarize the results in the pager below:

We first notice that when the bins used are restricted to 2-10, then the uncertainty on every parameter more than doubles, especially for the \(g\) parameter. We further notice that when restricting the bins from 2-10, the direction of the epsilon impact flips. This is due to the particular shape of g, which has smaller signal at lower multipoles and larger signal at higher multipoles between the ranges of ell=0 to 500.

Since the beam broadening effect is multiplicative, when we test the \(g=0\) dataset, changing epsilon has little impact on the resulting uncertainty. However, for the \(g=1\) dataset, there is a visible dependence on epsilon. The effect is larger when using BICEP3 data only vs all 4 maps, demonstrating the importance of the multicomponent analysis.

We note that the bias is approximately 20% for an epsilon of 0.05, which is still less than 1 sigma for an injected g of 1, and will be even less significant for the expected g, which is less than 0.2.

Only Scaling a Single Map Frequency

We perform the same test, but only scaling a single map rather than all 4 of the BK18 maps at the same time. We summarize the results in the pager below: From the summary plots, we see that the effect of epsilon on the g parameter is approximately halved when only a single map frequency is scaled compared to scaling every single map frequency. This makes sense intuitively because each map frequency covers about half of the cross maps (there are 16 total cross maps, and each frequency occurs in 7 of them). An interesting trend is that the K95 map scaling appears to cause the g parameter to drift in the opposite direction. This is likely because the K95 reobserved bandpowers are closer to zero, and the scaling can push the negative bandpowers even more negative, causing a negative bias in g.

Effect on Angle Difference

We perform the same test for the Delta Beta parameter with an ell break at bin 12. We summarize the results in the pager below:

We have similar results where we see the mean angle fits over 500 simulations to correlate with epsilon. Here, the Delta Beta parameters has a bias of approximately 0.1 degree for an epsilon of 5%, which is significant, but still within a single standard deviation. The effect essentially vanishes when looking at the simulations with no multipole dependence injected.

Conclusion

With a beam uncertainty in multipole of up to 5%, we see a small but significant impact on our multipole dependence parameters. The effects scale multiplicatively with the amount of injected multipole dependence in the simulation dataset, as shown by the zero correlation with epsilon for the standard lambda CDM sims. Given that our expected multipole dependence signal is close to zero, the beamshift uncertainty is expected to be a small percent of the overall uncertainty.
